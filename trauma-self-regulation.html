Trauma Recovery & Self-Regulation

Explore the science of trauma, learn what EMDR is, and practice gentle present-focused grounding skills. This learning space is not psychotherapy, a diagnosis, or an EMDR trauma-processing session.

Before you begin: You never need to describe, recall, or relive traumatic events here. Skip any exercise. If symptoms intensify, stop and orient to your surroundings. For severe distress, dissociation, or worsening symptoms, consult a qualified mental-health professional. If you are in immediate danger, call local emergency services; in the United States, call or text 988 for crisis support.

Learn the evidence and limitations

Lesson 1

Trauma responses

Threat responses can include hyperarousal, avoidance, intrusive memories, and changes in attention or mood. These symptoms do not mean every stressful experience leads to PTSD.

Research and counterarguments

Trauma response varies widely. Symptoms can overlap with other conditions; a qualified clinician considers duration, impairment, context, and alternative explanations. Not all trauma survivors need therapy.

Lesson 2

What EMDR actually involves

Clinician-delivered EMDR is a structured psychotherapy with history-taking, preparation, assessment, reprocessing, installation, body scan, closure, and reevaluation. Bilateral stimulation alone does not constitute full EMDR.

Evidence and debate

EMDR is recommended in major PTSD treatment guidelines. Debate continues over precisely how eye movements contribute, compared with other effective components of trauma-focused psychotherapy. Evidence for unguided EMDR trauma reprocessing is insufficient to treat it as equivalent.

Lesson 3

Grounding and regulation

Grounding focuses attention on the present. Slow, comfortable breathing, sensory orientation, and supportive connection can help some people with temporary distress, but effectiveness varies.

When to stop

Some students find body awareness, eyes-closed practices, or repetitive tapping uncomfortable. Choose eyes-open grounding or no exercise at all; stop if anxiety, flashbacks, or disconnection increase.

Lesson 4

Getting appropriate care

Trauma-focused CBT, cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure, and EMDR are evidence-based options for PTSD under professional guidance. Fit, access, and patient preference matter.

Critical thinking

Feeling calmer after an exercise is not proof that traumatic memories have been processed or PTSD has been treated. Self-help is best viewed as supplementary support.

Practice A · no memory recall

5–4–3–2–1 sensory orientation

With your eyes open, gently notice:

  1. Five things you can see.
  2. Four things you can physically feel around you.
  3. Three sounds you can hear.
  4. Two smells you notice (or two neutral colors).
  5. One taste or one comfortable point of contact with the floor.

Take your time; you can stop or change any prompt. There is no score and no requirement to finish.

Practice B · optional calming exercise

Butterfly Hug: gentle bilateral tapping

Cross your arms comfortably over your upper chest or rest your hands on your thighs. If comfortable, tap left and right alternately at your own pace while noticing the room around you. Keep your eyes open if preferred. Do not intentionally bring up traumatic memories during this exercise.

Optional 30-second visual timer only; it does not pace or prescribe taps. No sounds, flashing lights, or automatic eye-tracking are used.

Afterward, ask yourself only: “Do I feel more settled, less settled, or unchanged?” No response needs to be recorded. If you feel worse, stop and seek support.
